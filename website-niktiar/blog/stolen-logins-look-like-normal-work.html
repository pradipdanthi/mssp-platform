Stolen logins that look like normal work

The password was correct. The prompt was approved. That is often how the intrusion begins.

28 October 2026 · 3 min read · Kevantic Cyber Security

The password was correct. The sign-in prompt was approved. The person was at their desk. That is the shape of a large share of intrusions now, and it is why an alert that only says “login succeeded” tells you almost nothing.

Microsoft’s 2026 Digital Defense Report, covering July 2025 through June 2026, put phishing at 23 percent of observed intrusions, up from 7 percent the year before. Of the intrusions that already had a valid account, 52 percent went on to steal more credentials. The pattern is a quiet chain. Someone borrows a real session, then uses it to collect the next one.

Who feels this first

Finance teams feel it when an invoice thread grows a new rule that hides the vendor’s mail. Clinics feel it when a mailbox can reach scheduling and records. Factories feel it when a shared account can reach both email and the remote gateway into the plant. Anyone whose email approves money is in this group.

What good watching looks like

  • A sign-in from two places that a person cannot physically be in at once.
  • A burst of approval prompts until someone, tired, taps yes.
  • A new mail rule, a new forwarding address, or a new app the user does not remember allowing.
  • The same account touching systems it has never touched, in the hour after a normal morning login.

None of those require a wrong password. All of them are behaviour after a sign-in that looked fine.

Ask your current watcher one question: if the password is right, what do you still look for in the next hour? If the answer is “nothing,” the account is unguarded the moment it is stolen.
