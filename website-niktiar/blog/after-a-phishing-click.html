What actually happens after a phishing click

Training posters talk about the click. The loss usually happens in the hour after it.

28 October 2026 · 3 min read · Kevantic Cyber Security

People still picture phishing as a badly spelled email. A lot of it is now a small industry. One group builds the fake page. Another sends the mail. Another uses the session. The page can look exactly like a vendor the company already pays. Busy people approve it because it resembles work.

Training still matters. It is not the whole job. The expensive minutes start when the click has already happened.

The questions that decide the week

  • Did a mailbox rule appear that hides invoices, or forwards a copy outside the company?
  • Did a file share gain a guest who was not on Friday’s list?
  • Did a payment, a payroll change, or a patient record leave through an account that usually only reads?
  • Did a laptop start calling an address the company has never used?

Those are ordinary business questions. They are also the spine of a case. A poster that says “do not click” does not answer them.

What the case should feel like

A good response names an owner, freezes the parts that are still moving, and writes a short account a director can forward. The technical timeline stays with the people who work it. The customer hears what happened, what it means for operations, and what to do before the next payment run.

Kevantic Incident Response and Casework is that analyst-led case. The Cloud SOC stays with it. Identity watching, described in stolen logins that look like normal work, is often how the case starts. If the question later becomes “what exactly was touched,” the record has to still exist — that is the point of keeping evidence after the alert.

Why the same click looks different in a clinic and a factory

In a clinic, the hour after a click is about a mailbox that can see appointments and records. The business question is whether a message, a file, or a login was used to look at someone else’s care. In a factory, the same click is about whether that mailbox can approve a vendor change or open a remote session into the plant. In a finance team, it is about whether a payment instruction changed before anyone noticed the thread looked odd.

The case notes should follow that difference. A generic “phishing incident” label helps nobody in the morning meeting. The useful note says which business process was in reach, whether it moved, and what was stopped. Kevantic writes that note from the Cloud SOC. The raw mail and endpoint history that prove it stay on the NikTiar Edge Node.

If the only artefact of a click is a screenshot in a chat thread, you do not have a case. You have a rumour. A case has a timeline, an owner, and a decision.
